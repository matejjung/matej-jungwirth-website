About

Hello!

My name is Matěj [ma-tey] Jungwirth and I am a PhD candidate in Political Science at Northwestern University, and a Visiting Research Fellow with the Migration Institute of Finland.

In my doctoral dissertation, Losing Land, Not Its People, I develop a novel concept of territorial mourning — the society-wide response to the trauma of territorial loss and forced displacement. I argue that understanding the emergence, variation and persistence of territorial mourning is key in explaining present-day political outcomes in loss-incurring states. These legacies include public attitudes towards territorial compromises, public commemoration of the lost territories and foreign policies towards historical enemies.

Methodologically, my dissertation is guided by the logic of Comparative Historical Analysis; through a mixed-methods research approach, I draw on original surveys and survey experiments, archival research in Czech, Finnish and German, expert interviews and online content analysis.

Alongside my dissertation research and undergraduate teaching, I publish and study polarization in Central and Eastern Europe, return decision-making of displaced Ukrainians and conflict resolution attitudes in pre-2022 Ukraine.

Beyond academia, I discuss developments in US politics in media outlets in the Czech Republic and Slovakia as a Research Fellow with the Prague-based AMO think tank.

In my personal life, I greatly enjoy a variety of sports with my son including rock climbing, soccer and baseball, spending time in the great Midwestern outdoors and building a proper Finnish sauna.

Dissertation Research

Losing Land, Not Its People

In my doctoral dissertation (co-chaired by James Mahoney and Jordan Gans-Morse), I seek to explain the phenomenon and legacies of territorial mourning or how societies respond to the forced displacement of co-citizens engendered by the loss of national territories. I argue that across cases the developments in the immediate aftermath of territorial dispossession lastingly shape the nature and character of territorial mourning — which takes a distinctly different character for displaced citizens than for their non-displaced compatriots. The variation in the prevailing type of territorial mourning then influences the way lost territories are commemorated and instrumentalized at the national level. Different modes of territorial mourning lead to, in turn, different impacts on public attitudes, national policies and commemorative practices with regards to the lost territories and displaced populations.

Methodologically, my dissertation is a mixed-methods project following the overarching logic of Comparative Historical Analysis across three cases of territorial loss and large-scale dispossession of people. Across these cases, I weave together archival research, expert interviews, ethnographic fieldwork and original survey data collection to trace the emergence and influence of territorial mourning. Given the cross-country historical nature of my research, I read and study primary and archival sources in Czech, Finnish, and German for my dissertation.

Sudetenland

Czechoslovakia · 1938

Following the infamous 1938 Munich Agreement, the Czechoslovak republic was forced to give up some one fifth of its territory — the Sudetenland borderlands with large ethnic German populations — over to Nazi Germany. This territorial transfer engendered the displacement of some 370,000 Czechoslovak citizens from the ceded territories to the remainder of Czechoslovakia.

Methods: archival research of public administrative records in the Czech National Archive; public discourse analysis.

* “Small but ours”, map of truncated Czechoslovakia after the Munich agreement, 1938. Kolínská cikorka. Via Wikimedia Commons

Karelia

Finland · 1940/1944

Following its resolute defense in the Winter War, Finland ceded Karelia and additional eastern regions — some 11% of its territory — in 1940, a loss upheld despite temporary reclamation in 1941–1944. Some 420,000 Finnish citizens were resettled in the remainder of Finland through a mass state-led land redistribution effort.

Methods: archival research; expert interviews; local ethnography in Eastern Finland.

* Coats of arms of ceded Karelian municipalities, Karjalatalo, Helsinki. Photographed 2026

Nagorno-Karabakh

Armenia · 2023

The military offensive by Azeri forces in the fall of 2023 brought an end to nearly three decades of Armenia's control over the disputed mountainous territory of Nagorno-Karabakh. Following the change of sovereignty, some 120,000 Armenians fled the territory to the remainder of Armenia.

Methods: original survey fielded via CRRC-Armenia in May 2026 (n = 759). Study pre-registration

Working papers

  • Others Might Return but I Will Not

    I study refugees’ return decision-making using an original survey of displaced Ukrainians in the Czech Republic (n = 1,048) fielded in fall of 2024. Using an innovative paired conjoint design, I show that under the same hypothetical conditions, refugees assess the return intentions of other displaced individuals differently than their own. Study pre-registration

  • Proximity, Permeability, and the Possibility of Violence

    With Aaron Erlich and Calvin Garner. Using multiple survey waves fielded in Ukraine between 2015 and 2019, we show that citizens’ distance to different border regimes with Russia and Russia-occupied territories is associated with conflict resolution attitudes.

  • Structures in Our Heads: Social Identity and Party Attachments in Central and Eastern Europe

    With Tadeáš Cely and Lenka Hrbková. Using original survey data from the Czech Republic, Hungary and Lithuania (n = 4,296) and an embedded experiment, we argue that under conditions of party flux and weak social ties, identification with groups is highly individualized, and these “structures in our heads” strongly predict the role social groups play in party attachments and partisan affect.

Sauna

Inspiration

The Dream of Löyly

One of my foremost non-academic pursuits is the planning and building of a Finnish sauna. My yearning for löyly (sauna steam) originates in the two years I spent with my family in Helsinki, Finland, where sauna is not a luxury but a fundamental human right and, as many Finns would passionately argue, one of the foremost reasons why Finland was relatively unaffected during the COVID-19 pandemic.

I was originally planning on purchasing a pre-fab kit but in the end decided that I prefer the full freedom in terms of design and insulation choices that a full build offers. However, with great power comes great responsibility that I do not take lightly — cue hours spent perusing r/Sauna, Trumpkin's sauna notes and Lassi Liikkanen's excellent books on sauna design.

The goal for this sauna is to not only create a space where my family can unwind and relax but where we can also invite in our friends, family and neighbors for a regular session of heat and cold exchange; a much welcome respite in the thick of Midwestern winters. Thus, this section will serve as a building log with regular text and photo updates as the project advances.
